The first sign of the ego trip was unpunctuality. I'll tell you the other five when I get to know you better. It had been a wet, cold, lousy day all up. I'd been fired that morning from the proud position of moral guru of the Sydney Morning Herald. And I was in no mood for any further soggy delay.

The Wound

Memorably, Les once said that art is not born in you, it is grafted in you through a wound. The Church at Cranbrook

The first sacred site, and it was pretty sacred, was the church in Cranbrook where Murray regularly and brazenly worshipped the threefold God he said in his book had done precious little for him. A choice of belief and lifestyle I never understood.

You were drawn to Catholicism by what?

Les Murray

The pictures, partly. You could see it.

There were many reasons. Another thing was that it had two impossible possibilities. One was that God could turn into bread and wine, and the other one was that a man could rise from the dead and say that he'd bring all the rest of us out of it as well.

And I trust the impossible because that's where everything comes from. It's the only door that opens, you know. There was a time when life was impossible, and there was a time when flight was impossible, and there was a time when speech was impossible, a time when it was impossible to go to the moon.

I'll tell you about that business of transforming God into bread and wine. It wasn't hard for me to accept at all because I'd seen words transformed into poetry, which is very much the same thing, same sort of thing, you know, that same shift of dimension.

So you cannibalise the God on a regular basis.

Les Murray

Only at his invitation. Very primitive stuff, religion is. What it's saying is all those people who practiced cannibalism and other things in the past were not wrong, they were on the right track. And this is the track, you know, after which it doesn't have to be done anymore.

That's the one for the lightning, not me. As a Seventh-day Adventist, then I couldn't drink, smoke, dance, go to the pictures, pick flowers on Saturday—

Les Murray

Newcastle

The second sacred site, when Murray was off reading poetry to schoolchildren somewhere, was one of mine, a little brick house in Newcastle I lived in when I was first fugitive from grief.

I spent a month here when I was 10 and I turned 11 in this house. I was being looked after by my Auntie Jean and Uncle Ben after my sister Margaret was killed on Parramatta Road at the age of 22 and my mother became emotionally indisposed, and there was some thought then, I think, that I might have stayed here for good. In many ways, I wish I had. It's a kind of paradise on earth.

A paradise on earth in a little brick cottage on Monmouth Street, exactly the same as all the other little brick cottages of Stockton, a truly working class and truly egalitarian little island of democratic decency across the Hunter River from the steelworks, whose lights and sulfurous belches over the broad water by night were like a fairground, or the great alien ship in Close Encounters.

A lovely grimy Victorian town was doomed now, I decided, on Stockton Beach. A town the economically correct would soon turn into an archaeological relic, at one with Nineveh and Tyre, with the sandhills blowing back over it and the pelicans building nests in the mudflats that soon would conquer all.

Taree

His next place of memory was not so sacred for him, more like profane. A place of unfinished business and unlaid ghosts that reached out for him over 35 years and grabbed him by the throat and shook him.

Canberra

When did you first form a view of how you look?

Les Murray

Me? Oh, early in childhood, and I thought I looked soupy and soppy, you know, and kind of off the planet a bit. And I was wearing a hat and bare feet, and that was the wrong order. In the middle classes, you wore footwear and no hat. And then a fella called me Nugget, and I realised I was fat.

If you could harness that grief, you could light a city for a hundred years.

Les Murray

Oh, God, yeah, for a long, long time. Harnessing grief of this sort, though, can be dangerous. The Germans harnessed theirs and made another war out of it.

Never such innocence again? That's about it, yeah. You've got to hope that most of these died quick and clean, and you know that they didn't.

Old Parliament Steps

My final sacred site was old Parliament steps on which in 1975 I saw Gough Whitlam cry, maintain your rage. I knew in a frozen instant all was lost.

There I drank a bit with Les and grew, I fear, a trifle impatient.

You, Murray, are an idle bastard. You are supposed to be the unacknowledged legislator of history.

Les Murray

Unacknowledged, you see. If they acknowledged me, I would solve it all.

I'll acknowledge you. I'll make you poet laureate.

Les Murray

I don't want your poet laureate.

The man is insatiable.

Les Murray

What did your great master, Gore Vidal, say about the splendidly developed social welfare of this country?

He said, it's a wonderful country. You look after people so well — that old crippled couple, the Whitlams, for instance, down to their last cup of caviar — and how you do care for them.

I plan to manufacture you, Murray, as a soft toy. You pull the string and it goes—

Near Yass

We drove back and stopped near Yass, where Les, once aged 20, looked up at strange new hills and trees and rocks and realised he was in his first foreign country, somewhere other than home. And we had, like old competitive friends, a biennial game of pool, with the usual references to that long-loved film, The Hustler, and Fast Eddie Felson, and Minnesota Fats.

Les Murray

It does us good, I think, to try ourselves out at something in which we have no talent whatever.

What was the great movie of your life, Robert? Mine was From Here to Eternity. Got me through my awful teenage. I could recite every word of it at one time.

Mine was He Who Must Die, the story of the Christ. The Miracle Play. That was what taught me that movies could be an experience like, and better than, life itself. And they weren't just wallpaper in the mind.

Les Murray

Movies came out of the air. They didn't have actors in them or anything. They were made mystically somewhere. And I never cared who the actor's name was. But I knew every movie From Here to Eternity because it was my situation — despised and harassed and persecuted bugle player. But I was also the strong, masterful Sergeant Warden. I felt it all on my skin, that film.

The pool table is the great demonstration of the infinitude of human possibility. There is no pool game that resembles any other pool game. And yet there are only 15 variables. And a few feet. Imagine predicting life itself.

Les Murray

You can almost be proud to be no good at it, because it means you're not a dictator forcing people into— Is that not the airport?

Yeah, that's where I am. That's how good I am.

Les Murray

Can you play a game of billiards? Can you cannon off the push?

I can cannon off the shithouse, said the bastard from the bush.

Would you knock him down and rob him, said the captain of the push. I'd knock him down and fuck him, said the bastard from the bush. Would you have a cigarette, mate, said the captain of the push. I'll take the flame and pack it, said the bastard from the bush.

One of the great lines in all literature.

It's the small victories that are the best.

Back to Sydney

We visited at last, on getting back to Sydney, the places we first had known well when we lived at Bondi and Waverley, together in part. On the golf course we had slept on for a week, or was it only a night? As in the film I made about those years, The Nostradamus Kid. It didn't seem any time had passed at all, but of course it had, and time is fleeting.

What's it all about, Murray? Are we mere wriggling viruses under some cosmic microscope in a grand experiment that is nearing its end?

Les Murray

They have their dignity, perhaps. I don't know whether they wriggle or not. It's all very mysterious to me. I can't help thinking that it changes... It doesn't change, but we change our opinions about it all the time, our points of view.

Beazley, laughing, claimed he'd think about the laureateship when the film wound down and receded. And we each went back to our chosen calling of intellectual roustabout and poetical busker, Murray as usual among schoolchildren, explaining as always the unknowable origins of his most evocative and troubling work.

An Absolutely Ordinary Rainbow

Les Murray

A fella stood up in it, in my mind, who wanted to weep. You know, weep in Martin Place, weep in the most public space in Sydney. And all I had to do was make sure that I never attached his weeping to any particular cause. You see, the title says, Absolutely Ordinary Rainbow. He's an absolute. He's weeping. The people around him do exactly what you do when you're reading a poem. They have to make up their minds what it means to them, you know. And a mysterious poem — I don't understand it myself.

"An Absolutely Ordinary Rainbow" The word goes round Repins,
the murmur goes round Lorenzini's.
At Tattersall's, men look up from sheets of numbers,
the Stock Exchange scribblers forget the chalk in their hands
and men with bread in their pockets leave the Greek Club.
There's a fellow crying in Martin Place. They can't stop him.

The crowds are edgy with talk and more crowds come hurrying.
Many run in the back streets
which minutes ago were busy main streets, pointing:
There's a fellow weeping down there. No one can stop him.

The man no one approaches
simply weeps, and does not cover it,
weeps not like a child, not like the wind, like a man.
And uniforms back in the crowd who tried to seize him
stare out at him, and feel with amazement
their minds longing for tears as children for a rainbow.

Ridiculous, says a man near me,
and stops his mouth with his hands
as if it uttered vomit—
and I see a woman, shining, stretch her hand
and shake as she receives
the gift of weeping;
as many as follow her also receive it.

And when he stops, he simply walks between us,
mopping his face with the dignity of one
who has wept, and now has finished weeping.

Evading believers, he hurries off down Pitt Street.

Coda

And I went home with a new sense of old times and loss and gain, my family, visiting parrots, and Annie, my wife of 30 years, and allowed myself a prickly judgment or two on the one true immortal I would ever know well.

Murray and I are brothers, and with all the warring contumely that means, we have each other forever, whatever that means.

And I fear that Murray, in being only a poet, has failed in his duty to be also completely a citizen. I think that is so. I fear he has done too little with his life, and in doing only the poetry, he has missed out on some fraction of existence which would have done him good.

Les Murray

Well, my thanks to everyone concerned, particularly the actors. This is rather like attending a reading after my death.

Half-buried timbers, chain corduroy,
lead out into the sand
which bare feet, wincing, crutch and crotch,
spurn for the summer surf's embroidery
and insects stay up on the land.

A storm engrossing half the sky
in broccoli and seething drab
and standing on one foot
over the country burns like a lit torch.
Lightning turns air to elixir at every grab.

The younger sister wavering shouts, stay dead!
She knows how impossibility is the only door that opens.
She pities his fall, leg under one knee,
but her power is his death
and can't be dignified.

He's got the eyes. There's nothing in them, but that's the way they are.

Old man's hands, like hands on a deathbed.

You get past the tongue and the drool, and that's Murray all right. That's him.
